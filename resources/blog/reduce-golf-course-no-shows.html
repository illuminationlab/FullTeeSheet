Reduce golf course no-shows

Cutting No-Shows and Short Shows
at Your Course.

No-shows and short shows quietly eat your revenue. A simple reminder-and-confirm system reclaims those rounds. Here's how to build one.

You cut no-shows at a golf course with a reminder-and-confirm system: a short sequence of texts and emails that nudges every booked golfer to confirm, makes it easy to cancel early when plans change, and instantly backfills any slot that frees up from a waitlist. Build that once and the rounds that used to evaporate — the four-top that never showed, the twosome that arrived as a single — start coming back as revenue. Here's how each piece works.

1. No-shows vs. short shows — and why both cost you.

A no-show is obvious: a booked tee time nobody plays. But short shows are the quieter leak, and most courses never track them. A short show is the foursome that booked and arrived as a twosome, or the group that reserved but two players "couldn't make it." You held four spots, you collected two green fees, and the two empty spots were unsellable because they were still on your sheet as booked.

Both come from the same root cause: a booking made days ago that nobody re-confirmed. Plans changed, a player dropped, and your tee sheet never found out in time to do anything about it. Add the no-shows and the short shows together and the number of rounds walking out the door is bigger than the no-show column alone ever shows.

2. The confirmation cadence that actually works.

The fix is a confirmation cadence — and the timing matters more than the number of messages. Too early and the golfer forgets again; too late and there's no time to resell a freed slot. A cadence that works for most courses looks like this: a reminder a couple of days out that asks the golfer to confirm the whole group, then a shorter reminder the morning of the round.

The key move is asking golfers to confirm their headcount, not just their tee time. "Reply to confirm all 4 players" surfaces the short show days before it happens — the golfer replies "actually just 2 of us," and now those two open spots are back in your inventory with time to sell them. That single question converts an invisible short show into a reclaimable slot.

3. Make cancellation easy on purpose.

This one feels backwards, so it's worth saying plainly: you want to make canceling easy. A golfer who can cancel with one tap the moment their Saturday falls apart hands you a slot with two days' notice — enough time to resell it at full rate. A golfer who has to call during business hours, sit on hold, or feel awkward about it just doesn't show up. Now you have a dead slot and no warning.

Put a clear cancel or reschedule link in every reminder. The goal is to convert silent no-shows into early cancellations, because an early cancellation is a slot you can refill and a no-show is a slot you can't. The easier you make it to bail, the more warning you get — and warning is the whole ballgame.

4. Backfill with a waitlist the moment a golfer drops.

Making cancellation easy only pays off if you resell the freed slot fast, and that's what a waitlist does automatically. The instant a golfer cancels or trims their group, the system texts the golfers who wanted that time window and gives the slot to the first to claim it. No counter staff working a phone list, no window sitting open hoping someone calls.

This is where reminders and waitlists become one system instead of two features. The confirmation cadence frees the slot early; the waitlist resells it immediately. Run them together and a cancellation stops being a loss and becomes a swap. It's the same fill mechanic covered in our guide to filling empty tee times — pointed at your cancellations instead of your slow blocks.

5. Measure the reclaimed rounds.

The last piece is proof. Track three numbers: your no-show rate, your short-show rate (only visible once you're confirming headcounts), and the slots your waitlist resold. Watch them month over month and the impact of the system becomes a real figure you can put next to its cost — instead of a vague sense that "fewer people flake now."

A dashboard that surfaces those numbers without manual exports is what turns this from a guess into a decision.
